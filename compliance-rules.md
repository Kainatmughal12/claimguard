# ClaimGuard compliance rule pack

**27 rules across 7 categories.**

> **Read this first.** This is a simplified, plain-English rule pack built for a
> prototype. It is not legal advice and it is not a complete compliance
> reference. Real healthcare marketing review involves state-by-state variation,
> specialty-specific board rules, and a qualified compliance professional. Say
> this openly in your demo video and written note — naming the limitation is a
> stronger signal than pretending it isn't there.

---

## Category 1 — Treatment and outcome claims

### CLAIM-001 — Disease cure claims
**Rule:** Marketing must not state or imply that a service or product cures,
treats, or eliminates a disease or medical condition unless that claim is
supported by competent and reliable scientific evidence.
**Authority:** FTC Act §5; FDA disease-claim restrictions
**Severity:** high
**Violation:** "Our laser therapy cures chronic migraines."
**Fix:** "Our laser therapy is used to help manage chronic migraine symptoms.
Results vary by patient."

### CLAIM-002 — Implied permanence
**Rule:** Do not describe results as permanent, lifelong, or lasting forever
unless clinical evidence supports that duration.
**Authority:** FTC Act §5
**Severity:** high
**Violation:** "Permanent hair removal in six sessions."
**Fix:** "Long-lasting hair reduction. Most patients see results after a series
of sessions; maintenance may be needed."

### CLAIM-003 — Prevention claims
**Rule:** Claims that a service prevents disease require the same evidentiary
support as treatment claims and are held to a high standard.
**Authority:** FTC Act §5; FDA
**Severity:** high
**Violation:** "Regular cleanings prevent heart disease."
**Fix:** "Research suggests a link between oral health and cardiovascular
health. Regular cleanings support good oral hygiene."

### CLAIM-004 — Speed-of-result claims
**Rule:** Specific timeframes for results must reflect typical outcomes, not
best-case ones, and must be substantiated.
**Authority:** FTC Act §5
**Severity:** medium
**Violation:** "Straighter teeth in just 3 weeks."
**Fix:** "Treatment length varies by case; many patients complete treatment in
6–12 months."

### CLAIM-005 — Comparative efficacy
**Rule:** Claims that a service is more effective than an alternative require
head-to-head evidence, not general reasoning.
**Authority:** FTC Act §5
**Severity:** medium
**Violation:** "More effective than traditional braces."
**Fix:** "An alternative to traditional braces that some patients prefer for
comfort and appearance."

## Category 2 — Substantiation

### SUBS-001 — Unsourced statistics
**Rule:** Any specific statistic must have an identifiable source. Unattributed
numbers are treated as unsubstantiated.
**Authority:** FTC Act §5; FTC health products guidance
**Severity:** high
**Violation:** "97% of our patients report total pain relief."
**Fix:** Either cite the source and methodology, or remove the figure and
describe outcomes qualitatively.

### SUBS-002 — "Clinically proven"
**Rule:** The phrase "clinically proven" (and equivalents) requires reference to
actual clinical studies supporting the specific claim being made.
**Authority:** FTC Act §5
**Severity:** high
**Violation:** "Our clinically proven whitening system."
**Fix:** "Our whitening system uses [ingredient], which has been studied for
enamel safety." — with a citation, or drop the phrase.

### SUBS-003 — "Doctor recommended"
**Rule:** Claims of professional endorsement require a real, disclosable basis —
not the practice's own clinicians presented as independent authority.
**Authority:** FTC Act §5; FTC Endorsement Guides
**Severity:** medium
**Violation:** "Doctor recommended for sensitive skin."
**Fix:** "Recommended by our dermatologists for patients with sensitive skin."

### SUBS-004 — Vague scientific framing
**Rule:** Terms like "scientifically formulated," "advanced technology," or
"medical grade" imply evidentiary backing and should be avoidable or supported.
**Authority:** FTC Act §5
**Severity:** low
**Violation:** "Medical grade advanced skin renewal science."
**Fix:** Describe the actual treatment and what it does, without borrowed
scientific authority.

## Category 3 — Testimonials and endorsements

### TEST-001 — Atypical results without disclosure
**Rule:** A testimonial describing results better than typical must disclose
what typical results actually are — a generic disclaimer is not sufficient.
**Authority:** FTC Endorsement Guides (16 CFR Part 255)
**Severity:** high
**Violation:** "I lost 40 pounds in one month with their program!"
**Fix:** Include the testimonial alongside a clear statement of typical
outcomes, e.g. "Most patients lose 1–2 pounds per week."

### TEST-002 — Undisclosed material connections
**Rule:** If a person giving a testimonial was paid, given free or discounted
services, or has any other material connection to the practice, that must be
disclosed clearly and near the endorsement.
**Authority:** FTC Endorsement Guides (16 CFR Part 255)
**Severity:** high
**Violation:** An influencer post praising the clinic with no disclosure.
**Fix:** Add a clear, conspicuous disclosure such as "Paid partnership" or
"I received complimentary treatment."

### TEST-003 — Fabricated or composite testimonials
**Rule:** Testimonials must come from real patients describing their real
experience. Composites, actors, or invented quotes are prohibited.
**Authority:** FTC Endorsement Guides
**Severity:** high
**Violation:** A stock-photo "patient" with a written-in-house quote.
**Fix:** Use real patient testimonials with authorization, or drop the format.

### TEST-004 — Testimonials making treatment claims
**Rule:** A practice cannot use a testimonial to make a claim it could not make
directly. Patient words do not lower the substantiation bar.
**Authority:** FTC Act §5; FTC Endorsement Guides
**Severity:** high
**Violation:** "Dr. Lee's treatment cured my arthritis!"
**Fix:** Use testimonials that speak to experience and service rather than
clinical outcomes.

## Category 4 — Superlatives and credentials

### CRED-001 — Unverifiable superlatives
**Rule:** Claims like "best," "#1," "top-rated," or "leading" require an
objective, disclosable basis.
**Authority:** FTC Act §5; state medical/dental board advertising rules
**Severity:** medium
**Violation:** "The best dentist in Dallas."
**Fix:** "Rated 4.9 stars across 380 Google reviews." — if accurate — or
describe what makes the practice distinctive.

### CRED-002 — "Specialist" without board certification
**Rule:** Many state boards restrict "specialist" and "specializing in" to
clinicians holding recognised board certification in that specialty.
**Authority:** State medical/dental board advertising rules
**Severity:** high
**Violation:** "Our specialists in cosmetic dentistry."
**Fix:** "Our dentists have advanced training in cosmetic procedures."

### CRED-003 — Misleading credential presentation
**Rule:** Credentials, affiliations, and memberships must be current, accurately
named, and not presented in a way that implies more than they are.
**Authority:** State board advertising rules; FTC Act §5
**Severity:** medium
**Violation:** "Member of the American Academy of Cosmetic Dentistry" where
membership is lapsed or is a paid directory listing presented as an honour.
**Fix:** State the affiliation accurately and currently.

### CRED-004 — Award and ranking claims
**Rule:** Awards must name the awarding body and year, and must not be pay-to-
list awards presented as merit-based.
**Authority:** FTC Act §5; state board rules
**Severity:** medium
**Violation:** "Award-winning practice."
**Fix:** "Named Best of [City] by [Publication], 2025."

## Category 5 — Guarantees and risk language

### GUAR-001 — Outcome guarantees
**Rule:** Do not guarantee a clinical result. Individual outcomes vary and
guarantees are treated as deceptive.
**Authority:** FTC Act §5; state board advertising rules
**Severity:** high
**Violation:** "Guaranteed results or your money back."
**Fix:** Describe the satisfaction or refund policy without guaranteeing a
clinical outcome.

### GUAR-002 — "Painless" and absolute comfort claims
**Rule:** Absolute claims about the absence of pain or discomfort are not
supportable across patients.
**Authority:** FTC Act §5
**Severity:** medium
**Violation:** "Completely painless procedure."
**Fix:** "We use local anaesthetic and sedation options to keep you
comfortable."

### GUAR-003 — "No risk" / "completely safe"
**Rule:** No medical or cosmetic procedure is risk-free. Claims of zero risk or
complete safety are prohibited and may also omit material risk information.
**Authority:** FTC Act §5; FDA
**Severity:** high
**Violation:** "A completely safe procedure with no risks."
**Fix:** "A well-established procedure. Your provider will discuss risks and
whether it's right for you."

### GUAR-004 — Omission of material risk
**Rule:** Where a procedure carries significant known risks or requires
recovery, marketing must not present it in a way that omits that material
information.
**Authority:** FTC Act §5
**Severity:** medium
**Violation:** "Walk in on your lunch break, walk out with a new look."
**Fix:** Mention that a consultation is required and that recovery time varies.

## Category 6 — Patient privacy

### PRIV-001 — Patient photos without authorization
**Rule:** Using a patient's image — including before/after photos — for
marketing requires prior written HIPAA authorization. Consent to treatment is
not consent to marketing use.
**Authority:** HIPAA Privacy Rule (45 CFR 164.508)
**Severity:** high
**Violation:** Posting a before/after photo from a patient chart.
**Fix:** Obtain and retain signed marketing authorization before any use.

### PRIV-002 — Identifiable patient details
**Rule:** Names, ages, dates of service, conditions, or any combination that
could identify a patient must not appear in marketing without authorization.
**Authority:** HIPAA Privacy Rule
**Severity:** high
**Violation:** "Sarah, 34, came to us in March with severe TMJ..."
**Fix:** Remove identifiers, or obtain written authorization.

### PRIV-003 — Responding to online reviews
**Rule:** Responding publicly to a patient review must not confirm that the
person is a patient or disclose any treatment detail, even to correct an
inaccurate review.
**Authority:** HIPAA Privacy Rule
**Severity:** high
**Violation:** "We're sorry your root canal was uncomfortable, Mr. Hassan."
**Fix:** "Thank you for the feedback. Please contact our office manager so we
can discuss your experience directly."

## Category 7 — Required disclosures

### DISC-001 — Missing results-vary disclosure
**Rule:** Content showing or describing outcomes should carry a clear statement
that individual results vary.
**Authority:** FTC Endorsement Guides; FTC Act §5
**Severity:** low
**Violation:** A results-focused post with no qualifier.
**Fix:** Add "Individual results vary" prominently, not buried in fine print.

### DISC-002 — Before/after photo disclosures
**Rule:** Before/after images should disclose whether they are actual patients,
whether images are retouched or filtered, and the timeframe between them.
**Authority:** FTC Act §5
**Severity:** medium
**Violation:** An unlabelled before/after grid.
**Fix:** "Actual patient. Photos taken 6 months apart. Not retouched."

### DISC-003 — Pricing and offer terms
**Rule:** Promotional pricing must disclose material conditions — what's
included, exclusions, expiry, and whether it's a starting price.
**Authority:** FTC Act §5
**Severity:** medium
**Violation:** "Invisalign for $99/month!"
**Fix:** "Invisalign from $99/month with approved credit, 36-month term. Final
cost determined at consultation."

## Notes for your interview

Things worth being ready to say:

- **Why plain-English rules rather than statutory text:** the agent needs to
  reason about copy, not parse law. Plain-English rules with a cited authority
  give it something actionable while keeping the source traceable.
- **Why severity defaults live on the rule, not the finding:** the rule carries a
  baseline; the agent can adjust per context. This keeps behaviour predictable.
- **What you'd do with more time:** state-level variation, specialty-specific
  board rules, versioning on the rule pack so past reviews stay auditable, and a
  review workflow for a compliance professional to maintain rules without
  touching code.
- **Where this would fail:** novel phrasing that evades the categories, sarcasm
  or implication rather than direct claims, and images — the current version only
  reads text.
